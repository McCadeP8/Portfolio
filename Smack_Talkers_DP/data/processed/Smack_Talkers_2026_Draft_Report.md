# Smack Talkers 2026 Draft Report

## How the grades work

These are **preseason process grades**, not predictions carved in stone and not hindsight grades. Each team’s score uses the league app’s 2026 projections and auction model: 55% projected points from the best legal starting lineup, 20% full-roster depth, and 25% value efficiency. Snake value compares the selection number with the model’s overall rank; auction value compares the winning bid with the model price. Each component is ranked within its own 12-team draft, so the letter grade answers: *who used this room best on draft night?* Injuries, role changes, trades, waivers, and weekly lineup decisions will eventually matter more.

## Snake Draft: overall synopsis

The room opened with a classic running-back/wide-receiver land rush: through three rounds, 18 of 36 picks were RBs and 14 were WRs. Only 2 quarterbacks and 2 tight ends broke that pattern. The first quarterback was Josh Allen at 2.07; the first defense was Houston Texans at 8.04, and the first kicker was Brandon Aubrey at 8.01. That created a long middle stretch in which teams chose between completing receiver depth, starting the quarterback run, or paying the opportunity cost for a scarce tight end.

The strongest grades came from teams that paired a bankable early core with patience later. Carson Pearson and Colton Bleckner finished first and second in the model because their starting groups stayed elite without hollowing out the bench; McCade Pearson and Shane Jensen generated the largest positive pick-value totals. The bottom of the board was not caused by illegal or incomplete rosters—every team filled all 16 slots—but by stacking several reaches and entering the season with a weaker projected starting combination. The broad lesson is that the room rewarded selective aggression: paying up for an anchor worked, but repeated deviations from the board compounded quickly.

## Auction Draft: overall synopsis

The auction spent its $2,400 exactly, with every owner using the full $200. Wide receivers drew $966 (40.2%) and running backs $866 (36.1%); together they absorbed more than three quarters of all money. Tight ends took $251, quarterbacks $218, defenses $54, and kickers $45. The headline prices were Bijan Robinson $79, Jahmyr Gibbs $77, Christian McCaffrey $70, Brock Bowers $64, Ja'Marr Chase $61, Jaxon Smith-Njigba $60.

Nomination order was chaotic—premium stars, quarterbacks, and even a kicker appeared immediately—so the real story was budget discipline rather than round labels. Kyle Adams produced the best blend of starting power, depth, and price value; Dallin Keppner’s roster carried the most model surplus, while Kristin Adams paired a top-two RB unit with a top-three defense. The weaker grades generally came from one of two paths: paying above model at multiple premium spots, or preserving dollar value but failing to turn it into a strong enough starting lineup. This was a stars-and-scrubs auction, but the successful versions still found playable $1–$3 depth instead of treating the final roster spots as throwaways.

## Final take

Both drafts told the same larger story in different languages. The room was willing to pay heavily for true RB/WR anchors, but it did not blindly follow one position script: Brock Bowers changed the early snake geometry, quarterback nominations arrived early in the auction, and managers repeatedly made personal conviction bets. The best rosters were not simply the teams with the most stars or the most discounts. They were the teams that converted draft capital into a complete starting lineup while still leaving themselves several realistic bench outs. That is the standard these grades reward—and the part of the draft that will be most interesting to revisit after the 2026 season supplies actual outcomes.
